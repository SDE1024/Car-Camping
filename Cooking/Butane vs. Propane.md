For cooking, **both butane and propane work well**, but they have different strengths—especially if you’re using a portable stove or camping.

| Feature | **Butane** | **Propane** |
|---|---|---|
| Cold-weather performance | Poorer | **Much better** |
| Typical boiling point | ~31°F / −1°C | ~−44°F / −42°C [Engineering ToolBox](https://www.engineeringtoolbox.com/hydrocarbon-boiling-melting-flash-autoignition-point-density-gravity-molweight-d_1966.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com) |

| Portability | **Very convenient/lightweight** | Heavier/bulkier |
| Stove style | Compact tabletop stoves | Camping stoves, grills, larger burners |
| Heat potential | Plenty for normal cooking | Often used for **higher-output burners** |
| Best use | Warm-weather/simple cooking | **Cold weather, extended camping, heavy cooking** |

The biggest practical difference is **temperature**. Butane needs to vaporize before it can feed the burner, and ordinary n-butane boils at roughly **31°F**. Around freezing and below, pressure drops dramatically and a butane stove may become weak or stop working. Propane boils around **−44°F**, so it continues vaporizing in temperatures far below freezing. [Engineering ToolBox](https://www.engineeringtoolbox.com/hydrocarbon-boiling-melting-flash-autoignition-point-density-gravity-molweight-d_1966.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

In normal warm weather, you probably won't notice much difference in cooking quality. Butane tabletop stoves are convenient for frying, boiling noodles, hot pot, etc. Propane is more common when you want a larger camping stove—for example, some propane camp stoves provide **30,000 BTU/hr per burner**.
